# Orrery: figures checked against NASA sources

Checked on 15 November 2026 with the web fetch tool; the pages named in the notes below were read again on that date for the second revision. The tool returns a summary of each page made by a small model, not the raw page, so a quoted sentence is that model's reading of the page. Where two NASA pages disagree, both values are given. NASA pages change, and nothing re-reads them automatically.

Sources: the NSSDC planetary fact sheets (`https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/`, the comparison table and the sheet for each body and satellite system) and NASA Science pages (`https://science.nasa.gov/...`).

Rules used: diameters are twice the fact sheet's volumetric mean radius; gravity is the fact sheet's mean surface gravity (for the giants at 1 bar), which is higher than the equatorial figure many books quote; distances of moons are the fact sheet's semimajor axis rounded to four figures.

How a figure is labelled: every figure on the page is either quoted from a NASA page (the Source value column gives the words and the page), or it is a fact sheet value rounded, or it is converted or computed from one, and then the section "Computed or converted, still on the page" below says from what. The light travel times and the ratios that earlier were computed (the Sun's light to Earth and Neptune, Mercury's spin against its orbit, Jupiter's mass against the other planets, Phobos against Mars's day, the width of Saturn's rings, a quarter of Uranus's orbit) were replaced in the second revision by sentences NASA gives, or removed; the Changed column says what each was. The page itself says, in its Credits panel, that a diameter is twice NASA's radius and that hours and days are converted, and gives the moon counts with their pages and the date they were read.

## Computed or converted, still on the page

Nothing here is a claim of NASA's; each is arithmetic on a value quoted above. The page's Credits panel says so for the diameters and for hours and days.

- Diameters: twice the fact sheet's volumetric mean radius (every planet and moon row, and the Sun).
- Rotation periods and day lengths: the fact sheet's hours turned into days (Mercury 1407.6 h = 58.65 days, Venus 5832.6 h = 243.0 days) or into hours and minutes (Earth 23.9345 h = 23 h 56 min, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune). NASA's own pages round differently (Mercury "59 Earth days").
- Moon orbital periods given in hours or minutes: the satellite fact sheet's days times 24 (Phobos 0.31891 d = 7 h 39 min, Deimos 1.26244 d = 30.3 hours, Mimas 0.9424218 d = 22.6 hours).
- Moon distances: the fact sheet's semimajor axis, rounded to four figures.
- Planet and moon positions at the date shown: computed by the page from the J2000 mean longitude and the orbit period in the table above; the orbits are drawn as circles in one plane, so a position is approximate.
- Counts of what the page draws ("20 major moons", "8" planets).

## Not verified, or verified only in part

- The Sun's age: the Sun facts page says "Our Sun is a 4.5 billion-year-old yellow dwarf star" and, in a later section, "The Sun formed about 4.6 billion years ago". The page shows 4.5 billion.
- Mimas, Tethys and Miranda diameters are within 1 to 2 km of the mean of the fact sheet's three radii and were left as they were.
- One NASA page could not be fetched (`science.nasa.gov/saturn/moons/enceladus/facts/`, client error); the Enceladus overview page was used instead.
- The short taglines under each name (for example "The cold red desert") are descriptions, not figures, and were not checked one by one beyond the facts listed above.
- The statements that a map "covers only the southern half" (Miranda) or "only part" (Triton) describe the NASA image files themselves.
